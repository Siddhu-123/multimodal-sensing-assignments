\begin{abstract}
Wearable human activity recognition relies on body-worn inertial sensors to classify physical behaviours for remote health monitoring. Single-sensor placements suffer from anatomical blind spots where distinct activities produce similar local kinematic signatures. This study investigates multimodal sensor fusion across acceleration and angular velocity modalities using the adapted MHEALTH dataset (343,195 samples across 10 participants). An audit identifies 615,648 missing values (7.80\%), where 90.4\% occur as isolated single-sample gaps, consistent with a Missing Completely at Random mechanism resolved via segment-bounded interpolation. Under a subject-independent evaluation (Subjects 1--7 training, Subject 8 validation, Subjects 9--10 testing), we benchmark three unimodal multi-layer perceptrons against feature-level early fusion and decision-level late soft voting. Late fusion achieves 95.56\% test accuracy and 0.9568 Macro-F1, outperforming the strongest unimodal baseline (chest acceleration, 0.8848 F1) by 7.20 percentage points and removing major confusions between walking and stair climbing, though static sitting (0.685 F1) and standing (0.807 F1) remain challenging. On-node feature extraction is estimated to reduce transmission payload by 86.2\% relative to raw telemetry, supported by an ethical governance framework.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Dataset Provenance and Architecture}
The Mobile HEALTH (MHEALTH) dataset, introduced by Ba{\~n}os et al.~\cite{banos2014mhealthdroid,banos2015sensor} and hosted on the UCI Machine Learning Repository~\cite{mhealth_uci_2014}, provides multimodal motion and physiological recordings for human activity benchmark analysis. Ten healthy volunteers of diverse physical profile performed 12 physical activities in an out-of-laboratory setting.

Data was recorded using three Shimmer2 wearable inertial units attached to the chest, right wrist, and left ankle at a sampling frequency of $f_s = 50\,\text{Hz}$ ($\Delta t = 20\,\text{ms}$), which satisfies Nyquist sampling criteria for voluntary movement ($<20\,\text{Hz}$)~\cite{bulling2014tutorial}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Chest Sensor:} Measures trunk tilt, gravitational alignment, and respiratory/cardiac motion via a tri-axial accelerometer ($\text{m/s}^2$) and 2-lead electrocardiogram (ECG, $\text{mV}$).
    \item \textbf{Left-Ankle Sensor:} Captures heel-strike impacts, propulsion, and stride frequency via a tri-axial accelerometer ($\text{m/s}^2$), tri-axial gyroscope (dimensionless sensor units), and tri-axial magnetometer~\cite{atallah2011sensor}.
    \item \textbf{Right-Wrist Sensor:} Captures forearm rotational rate, arm swing, and gestures via tri-axial accelerometer, gyroscope, and magnetometer.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Dataset Properties and Curation}
An inspection of the dataset identifies three structural properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Activity Class Scope:} In the benchmark recordings, unlabelled transition intervals between exercises accounted for over 70\% of raw samples. This work focuses on the 12 target physical activities ($L_1$ to $L_{12}$).
    \item \textbf{Sample Distributions:} The dataset contains 343,195 rows across 10 subject files (\texttt{mHealth\_subject1.log} to \texttt{mHealth\_subject10.log}). Timed activities ($L_1$--$L_5, L_9$--$L_{11}$) contain exactly 30,720 samples across participants ($\approx 61.4\,\text{s}$ per subject at 50\,Hz). Repetition-based tasks show cadence variation ($L_6$: 28,315; $L_7$: 29,441; $L_8$: 29,337). Jumping ($L_{12}$) contains 10,342 samples ($\approx 20.7\,\text{s}$ per subject), reflecting brief, explosive execution (Table~\ref{tab:activities}).
    \item \textbf{Sequence Permutation:} Activity execution order varies across participants (e.g., Subject 1 completes $L_5$ last, whereas Subject 8 swaps $L_2$ and $L_3$). Windowing and interpolation must therefore be bounded within individual activity segments to prevent cross-condition contamination.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Exploratory Data Analysis: Kinematics and Synergy}
We examine multi-axis kinematics across activity regimes to characterise pattern separability and sensor synergy.

\subsubsection{Signal Dynamics Across Activity Regimes}
Figure~\ref{fig:waveforms} contrasts raw sensor waveforms across three physical regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Static Posture (Standing, $L_1$):} Signals show stationary profiles dominated by gravity. The Chest Accelerometer records $\approx -9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$ along the vertical axis, while Ankle and Wrist sensors register near-zero dynamic deviation ($<0.05\,\text{m/s}^2$) and low angular rates ($\text{VM} \approx 1.0$).
    \item \textbf{Periodic Locomotion (Walking, $L_4$):} Displays sinusoidal patterns at a gait frequency of $\approx 1.8\,\text{Hz}$. Ankle acceleration exhibits dynamic swings ($[-15, +25]\,\text{m/s}^2$) from cyclic heel-strike impacts, while the Wrist Gyroscope captures smooth periodic oscillations from arm swing.
    \item \textbf{Explosive Impact (Jumping, $L_{12}$):} Features intermittent landing spikes ($>30\,\text{m/s}^2$) on the ankle, followed by aerial micro-gravity phases ($\approx 0\,\text{m/s}^2$) and sharp wrist rotations.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Movement Intensity and Inter-Subject Variance}
We compute instantaneous Vector Magnitude (VM) and Signal Magnitude Area (SMA) for each window:
\begin{align}
    \text{VM}(t) &= \sqrt{x(t)^2 + y(t)^2 + z(t)^2} \\
    \text{SMA} &= \frac{1}{W}\sum_{t=1}^W \sum_{k} |s_k(t)|
\end{align}
As shown in Figure~\ref{fig:energy_dist}, ankle acceleration magnitude separates physical intensity tiers, whereas wrist angular velocity magnitude maintains a narrow median across all activities ($\approx 1.0$--$1.2$ sensor units) and serves instead through directional and temporal dynamics. Across participant medians, ankle VM delineates:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sedentary and Low-Intensity Tiers ($L_1$--$L_3, L_6$--$L_8$):} Ankle acceleration reflects gravitational equilibrium, with median values between $9.1$ and $9.5\,\text{m/s}^2$.
    \item \textbf{Transitional and Moderate Ambulation ($L_5, L_9, L_4$):} Stair climbing ($L_5$) averages $10.0\,\text{m/s}^2$ and cycling ($L_9$) averages $9.7\,\text{m/s}^2$ (remaining low due to seated foot rotation), while walking ($L_4$) reaches $12.6\,\text{m/s}^2$ from heel impacts.
    \item \textbf{Vigorous and Explosive Regimes ($L_{12}, L_{10}, L_{11}$):} Jumping ($L_{12}$) produces $14.9\,\text{m/s}^2$, jogging ($L_{10}$) reaches $16.3\,\text{m/s}^2$, and running ($L_{11}$) peaks at $19.7\,\text{m/s}^2$.
\end{enumerate}
The stripplot points in Figure~\ref{fig:energy_dist} highlight substantial inter-subject variability; for example, during running ($L_{11}$), Subject 3 exhibits 31\% higher ankle acceleration than Subject 4 ($22.6\,\text{m/s}^2$ vs $17.3\,\text{m/s}^2$), reflecting personal cadence and foot-strike mechanics.

\subsubsection{Cross-Modality Correlation and Complementarity}
Figure~\ref{fig:correlation} presents the Pearson correlation matrix across 17 sensor channels ($N=50,000$). Ankle acceleration axes are weakly correlated ($r = -0.08$ X-Z, $0.06$ X-Y, $0.15$ Y-Z), indicating independent spatial axes. The strongest channel correlations occur between Ankle-GyrX and Wrist-GyrZ ($r = 0.60$) and between ECG Leads 1 and 2 ($r = 0.52$).
Cross-modality relationships demonstrate partial complementarity:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wrist Gyroscope channels exhibit correlations up to $|r| = 0.50$ with chest and ankle accelerometers (e.g., Ankle-AccZ vs Wrist-GyrY), capturing coupled limb movements rather than complete statistical independence.
    \item ECG Leads 1 and 2 show near-zero linear correlation ($|r| \le 0.07$) with all motion channels, operating on distinct physiological time scales.
\end{itemize}
This partial redundancy indicates that combining these modalities expands decision boundaries and mitigates unimodal blind spots~\cite{castanedo2013review}.

\subsection{Quantification and Diagnosis of Missingness}
Missing entries in wearable sensor streams arise from transmission dropouts, buffer overruns, or recording faults. Missing entries in the raw records are represented as IEEE \texttt{NaN} values.

An audit reveals \textbf{615,648 missing values} across 343,195 time steps, yielding an overall missingness rate of \textbf{7.80\%} across the 23 sensor channels (Table~\ref{tab:missing_stats}). The activity label column contains zero missing values. Missingness varies across channels (Figure~\ref{fig:missing_preproc}(a)), from 1.13\% (Wrist Gyro-Y) to 14.67\% (Ankle Mag-Z), but remains highly uniform across participants (7.75\% to 7.84\%) and across activities (7.72\% to 7.84\%).

\subsubsection{Burst Length Analysis and Missingness Mechanism}
To diagnose the missingness mechanism, we analyse consecutive missing sample runs (burst length $\ell = t_{\text{end}} - t_{\text{start}} + 1$). Figure~\ref{fig:missing_preproc}(b) shows that \textbf{90.4\% of bursts consist of a single sample} ($\Delta t = 20\,\text{ms}$). Bursts of $\ell = 2$ samples comprise 8.5\%, with a maximum burst length of $\ell_{\max} = 7$ samples ($140\,\text{ms}$) across the entire dataset. The mean burst length is $1.11 \pm 0.35$ samples.

The occurrence of missing entries is independent of signal magnitude and participant activity ($P(\text{missing} \mid \mathbf{x}, y) = P(\text{missing})$), with isolated single-sample losses dominating (90.4\%). This pattern conforms to the \textbf{Missing Completely at Random (MCAR)} mechanism~\cite{little2019statistical}.

\subsection{Segment-Bounded Intra-Run Interpolation}
Because voluntary human movement has mechanical bandwidth $<20\,\text{Hz}$, kinematic trajectories remain smooth over sub-150\,ms intervals. \textbf{Local linear interpolation} provides an accurate reconstruction:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{s}(t) = s(t_a) + \frac{t - t_a}{t_b - t_a}\left(s(t_b) - s(t_a)\right), \quad t_a < t < t_b
\end{equation}
where $t_a$ and $t_b$ are the nearest observed samples.

\textbf{Preventing Boundary Leakage:} Interpolating across raw file boundaries would mix adjacent activities (e.g., interpolating jumping impacts into stair climbing). To avoid boundary mixing, our pipeline enforces \textbf{segment-bounded intra-run grouping}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Partition subject logs into contiguous activity runs: $\mathcal{R}_k = \{(\mathbf{x}_t, y_t) \mid y_t = y_{t-1}\}$.
    \item Execute linear interpolation within each run $\mathcal{R}_k$.
    \item Resolve initial/final segment missing values via intra-segment boundary replication (\texttt{limit\_direction=both}).
\end{enumerate}
Figure~\ref{fig:missing_preproc}(c) shows that this approach reconstructs acceleration waveforms without cross-activity leakage. Post-processing confirms \textbf{zero residual missing values} (Table~\ref{tab:missing_stats}).

\subsection{Temporal Segmentation and Feature Engineering}
Wearable signals must be windowed to capture temporal structure while supporting real-time inference.

\subsubsection{Window Size and Overlap}
We apply a sliding window of $W = 128$ samples ($T = 2.56\,\text{s}$ at $50\,\text{Hz}$) with 50\% overlap ($\Delta W = 64$ samples, $1.28\,\text{s}$):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gait Periodicity:} Walking cadences range from 1.5 to 2.2 steps/s (stride time $\approx 0.9$--$1.3\,\text{s}$). A 2.56-second window captures over two full strides, supporting statistical stationarity.
    \item \textbf{Radix-2 FFT Efficiency:} Setting $W = 128 = 2^7$ optimises Fast Fourier Transform execution without padding artefacts.
    \item \textbf{Inference Latency:} A 1.28-second step provides responsive tracking suitable for health monitoring.
\end{enumerate}
Windows are extracted within each activity run $\mathcal{R}_k$, avoiding boundary crossing and producing $N = 5,229$ segmented windows.

\subsubsection{Extracted Features}
For each 3-axis sensor stream $\mathbf{S} \in \mathbb{R}^{128 \times 3}$, we extract 53 features:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Time-Domain Statistics (33 dims):} Mean $\mu$, standard deviation $\sigma$, variance $\sigma^2$, min, max, peak-to-peak range, median, interquartile range (IQR), skewness, kurtosis, and root mean square (RMS) per axis.
    \item \textbf{Movement Intensity (5 dims):} Mean, standard deviation, min, and max of the Vector Magnitude $\text{VM}(t)$, plus Signal Magnitude Area ($\text{SMA}$).
    \item \textbf{Axis Correlation (3 dims):} Pairwise Pearson coefficients $\rho_{xy}, \rho_{xz}, \rho_{yz}$.
    \item \textbf{Frequency-Domain Features (12 dims):} Computed via real-valued FFT ($N_{\text{bins}} = 65$, $\Delta f = 0.39\,\text{Hz}$). Extracts Spectral Energy $E$, Spectral Entropy $H$, Dominant Frequency $f_{\text{dom}}$, and Peak Amplitude per axis.
\end{itemize}
Extracting 53 features across three sensor sources yields a 159-dimensional multimodal feature space per window.

\subsubsection{Standardisation}
All features are standardised via Z-score scaling ($z = (x - \mu) / \sigma$). Parameters $\mu_{\text{train}}$ and $\sigma_{\text{train}}$ are computed on the Training split (Subjects 1--7) and applied to Validation and Testing sets, ensuring no test set leakage.

\subsection{Sensor Selection and Modality Justification}
We select three sensor sources spanning two physical modalities:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Chest Accelerometer (Linear Acceleration, 3 axes; Cols 0--2):} Measures upper-body posture, inclination during waist bending ($L_6$), and vertical displacement during jumping ($L_{12}$).
    \item \textbf{Left-Ankle Accelerometer (Linear Acceleration, 3 axes; Cols 5--7):} Measures ground impact shocks and lower-limb cadence, separating walking ($L_4$), climbing stairs ($L_5$), and running ($L_{11}$).
    \item \textbf{Right-Wrist Gyroscope (Angular Velocity, 3 axes; Cols 17--19):} Measures forearm rotational rates, identifying arm elevations ($L_7$), waist twists, and arm swings during cycling and running.
\end{enumerate}
This selection provides kinematic coverage across trunk, lower extremity, and upper extremity.

\subsection{Subject-Independent Experimental Design}
Randomly partitioning windows across subjects leaks personal biomechanical traits between splits, inflating performance estimates. We enforce a \textbf{Subject-Independent Partition}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training Set:} Subjects 1 to 7 ($N_{\text{train}} = 3,686$ windows, 70.5\%).
    \item \textbf{Validation Set:} Subject 8 ($N_{\text{val}} = 508$ windows, 9.7\%).
    \item \textbf{Test Set:} Subjects 9 and 10 ($N_{\text{test}} = 1,035$ windows, 19.8\%).
\end{itemize}
All models are evaluated on this identical test split under uniform preprocessing.

\subsection{Unimodal Baselines and Fusion Strategies}
We implement five model configurations in PyTorch:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline A (Chest Accelerometer):} Unimodal MLP on 53 chest features.
    \item \textbf{Baseline B (Ankle Accelerometer):} Unimodal MLP on 53 ankle features.
    \item \textbf{Baseline C (Wrist Gyroscope):} Unimodal MLP on 53 wrist features.
    \item \textbf{Early Fusion (Strategy 1):} Concatenates features into $\mathbf{x}_{\text{early}} = [\mathbf{f}_{\text{chest}} \,\|\, \mathbf{f}_{\text{ankle}} \,\|\, \mathbf{f}_{\text{wrist}}] \in \mathbb{R}^{159}$. A wider MLP (Table~\ref{tab:models}) learns cross-modal interactions in its early layers.
    \item \textbf{Late Fusion (Strategy 2):} Combines probability outputs from the three trained unimodal models via validation-weighted soft voting:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{p}_{\text{late}}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{m} w_m \mathbf{p}_m(\mathbf{x}), \quad \sum w_m = 1
    \end{equation}
    where weights reflect validation Macro-F1 on Subject 8: $w_{\text{chest}} = 0.358$, $w_{\text{ankle}} = 0.395$, and $w_{\text{wrist}} = 0.247$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Training and Convergence Diagnostics}
All models were trained for 45 epochs using AdamW ($1 \times 10^{-4}$ weight decay), gradient clipping at 2.0, and Cosine Annealing learning rate decay ($10^{-3}$ to $10^{-5}$). Model checkpointing and hyperparameter decisions were driven by validation performance on Subject 8.

Figure~\ref{fig:training_curves} confirms stable convergence:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Training and validation loss curves decrease smoothly and plateau after Epoch 25.
    \item Peak validation F1 on Subject 8 occurs at Epoch 18 for Early Fusion (0.8624), Epoch 19 for Ankle (0.8370), Epoch 11 for Wrist (0.5235), and Epoch 8 for Chest (0.7575).
    \item Validation loss does not diverge, demonstrating regularisation effectiveness.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Per-Class Performance and Error Analysis}
Table~\ref{tab:per_class_f1} provides a per-class F1 breakdown across all 12 classes for all five models. Combined with Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_matrices}, these results show how fusion resolves unimodal errors:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Ambulatory Confusion ($L_4$ Walking vs $L_5$ Stairs):} Chest Accel achieves only 0.676 F1 on walking, recalling 51\% of walking windows while misclassifying 49\% as stairs ($L_5$) due to similar vertical trunk motion. Ankle Accel achieves \textbf{1.000 F1} on walking because heel strikes and dorsiflexion kinematics differ from stairs. Early and Late Fusion both achieve \textbf{1.000 F1} on walking and stairs.
    \item \textbf{Upper-Limb Gestures ($L_6$ Waist Bends vs $L_7$ Arm Elevation):} Wrist Gyro achieves only $F_1 = 0.522$ on waist bends, as hands remain fixed relative to the trunk. Chest Accel achieves \textbf{1.000 F1} due to forward pitch. Conversely, Ankle Accel achieves only 0.739 F1 on arm elevation ($L_7$) because the lower body is motionless; Wrist Gyro scores 1.000 F1. Fusion resolves both cases, scoring \textbf{1.000 F1}.
    \item \textbf{Explosive Impacts ($L_{12}$ Jumping):} Wrist Gyro achieves $F_1 = 0.545$ on jumping, confusing arm recoil with general movement. Chest and Ankle Accelerometers record clear impact spikes ($F_1 = 1.000$), enabling fusion models to achieve \textbf{1.000 F1}.
    \item \textbf{Sitting ($L_2$) vs Standing ($L_1$):} The primary residual confusion across models is between Sitting ($L_2$, F1 = 0.685) and Standing ($L_1$, F1 = 0.807). Because the trunk remains vertical ($1\,\text{g}$ along the vertical axis) and both sensors are stationary, distinction relies on subtle differences in posture and wrist rest angle. The unimodal wrist baseline attains higher F1 on sitting (0.694) and standing (0.880) than late fusion, as forearm resting inclination provides discriminatory static cues.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{ROC and Precision-Recall Dynamics}
Figure~\ref{fig:roc_pr} shows macro-average ROC and Precision-Recall curves:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Early Fusion achieves an Area Under the ROC Curve of $\text{AUC} = 0.999$ while Late Fusion reaches $\text{AUC} = 1.000$ (Figure~\ref{fig:roc_pr}(a)), with true positive rates $>0.96$ at false positive rates below 0.01.
    \item Under class imbalance ($L_{12}$ has fewer samples), Precision-Recall curves (Figure~\ref{fig:roc_pr}(b)) confirm fusion advantages: Late Fusion attains $\text{AUC-PR} = 0.994$ and Early Fusion attains $0.982$, outperforming Chest (0.958), Ankle (0.915), and Wrist (0.887).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Trade-offs: Early vs Late Fusion}
The two fusion paradigms present distinct architectural trade-offs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early Fusion:} Learning joint cross-sensor interactions directly from concatenated features yields a compact unified model (76,172 parameters). However, inference strictly requires complete feature vectors across all three anatomical locations, making the pipeline vulnerable to single-node communication dropouts.
    \item \textbf{Late Fusion:} Unimodal networks operate independently, enabling modular fault tolerance where active prediction weights can be re-normalised dynamically if a sensor disconnects. Late Fusion attains the highest classification performance (0.9568 Macro-F1) while preserving modality-specific representations prior to decision integration.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Computational Implications for Edge Deployment}
Deploying multimodal inference on resource-constrained microcontrollers (e.g., ARM Cortex-M4) introduces specific constraints:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Memory Footprint:} Unimodal MLPs require 16,332 parameters ($\approx 65.3\,\text{KB}$ in float32). Early Fusion scales to 76,172 parameters ($\approx 304.7\,\text{KB}$), while Late Fusion combines three networks totaling 48,996 parameters ($\approx 196.0\,\text{KB}$) (Figure~\ref{fig:computational_tradeoffs}(a)), fitting within typical 512\,KB SRAM budgets.
    \item \textbf{Inference Latency:} Benchmarking 1,000 single-window passes on CPU yields \textbf{0.030\,ms for unimodal baselines}, \textbf{0.031\,ms for Early Fusion}, and \textbf{0.095\,ms for Late Fusion} (Figure~\ref{fig:computational_tradeoffs}(b)). Late Fusion evaluates three networks sequentially, but 0.095\,ms uses $<0.01\%$ of the 1,280\,ms window budget.
    \item \textbf{Bandwidth and Energy:} Wireless radio transmission consumes substantially more power than local microcontroller computation. Transmitting raw 50\,Hz data requires $128 \times 3 \times 4 = 1,536\,\text{bytes}$ per sensor per window. Extracting 53 features locally reduces telemetry payload to $53 \times 4 = 212\,\text{bytes}$ per sensor window---\textbf{an estimated 86.2\% payload reduction}.
    \item \textbf{Clock Synchronisation:} Early fusion requires sub-millisecond time alignment across nodes. Late fusion is more tolerant of arrival jitter, as unimodal decisions can be aggregated with timestamp buffers.
\end{enumerate}

Deploying continuous wearable sensing in clinical environments presents ethical, legal, and operational considerations (Table~\ref{tab:ethics_matrix}).

\subsection{Biometric Privacy Frameworks}
Wearable motion data contains identifying signatures. Prior biomechanical research indicates that tri-axial gait acceleration can support user identification, while ECG traces expose cardiac conditions and autonomic state~\cite{bulling2014tutorial}.

These data streams require structured governance:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{GDPR:} Under GDPR Article~4(14)~\cite{gdpr2016}, gait acceleration constitutes biometric data, while cardiac measurements fall under \textbf{Special Category Health Data (Article~9)}, requiring explicit consent (Article~6(1)(a) and 9(2)(a)). Article~22 restricts automated decision-making without clinical oversight.
    \item \textbf{HIPAA:} In clinical settings, wearable telemetry constitutes Protected Health Information (PHI), requiring encryption under the HIPAA Security Rule (45 CFR Part 164)~\cite{hipaa1996}.
    \item \textbf{Australian Privacy Principles (APPs):} Under the Privacy Act 1988, biometric and health data are classified as \textbf{Sensitive Information} (APP~3), requiring express consent and secure data lifecycle handling (APP~11)~\cite{oaic_app_2021}.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Algorithmic Bias and Demographic Generalisation}
The MHEALTH dataset was collected from 10 healthy adult participants, creating clinical generalisation risks:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
    \item \textbf{Age Biomechanics:} Older adults exhibit reduced stride length, slower cadence, and altered balance sway; models trained on young cohorts risk misclassifying normal elderly gait as abnormal.
    \item \textbf{Mobility Impairments:} Pathologies like post-stroke hemiplegia or Parkinsonian tremors alter movement dynamics, increasing false alarms without adaptive fine-tuning on clinical cohorts.
\end{itemize}

\section{Limitations and Future Improvements}
\label{sec:limitations}
Technical limitations and future research avenues include:
\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*,nosep]
    \item \textbf{Cross-Subject Evaluation:} The subject-independent split provides an established validation protocol; extending evaluation to leave-one-subject-out cross-validation across all 10 participants would further quantify variance across individual kinematic signatures.
    \item \textbf{Sitting vs Standing Resolution:} Confusion between Sitting ($L_2$) and Standing ($L_1$) remains the primary residual error (Table~\ref{tab:per_class_f1}). Adding a thigh-mounted IMU would directly capture femoral orientation ($90^\circ$ difference), resolving this ambiguity.
    \item \textbf{Deep Temporal Architectures:} While engineered statistical features support ultra-low edge latency, end-to-end sequential architectures (e.g., DeepConvLSTM~\cite{ordonez2016deep} or multimodal convolutional networks~\cite{radu2018multimodal}) could learn hierarchical spatio-temporal dynamics directly from raw waveforms.
    \item \textbf{Sensor Dropout Stress-Testing:} Evaluating classifier degradation under simulated packet loss (10\%--50\%) would benchmark the operational fault tolerance of modular late fusion under unstable wireless channels.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This work presented an end-to-end multimodal classification pipeline on the MHEALTH benchmark. Using segment-bounded interpolation, we resolved 615,648 missing values without cross-activity leakage. Evaluating across acceleration and gyroscope modalities on unseen test participants, Late Fusion achieved 95.56\% accuracy and 0.9568 Macro-F1, outperforming unimodal baselines and eliminating major ambulatory confusions, though static sitting and standing remain challenging. Edge profiling confirmed an estimated 86.2\% telemetry payload reduction via local feature extraction, supported by a governance framework aligned with international privacy standards.
