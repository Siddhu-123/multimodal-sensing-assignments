\begin{abstract}
Wearable Human Activity Recognition (HAR) using inertial sensors is central to remote patient monitoring and mobile health. However, individual sensor locations often fail to distinguish complex activities due to anatomical blind spots and sensor noise. This report evaluates multimodal data fusion techniques on the adapted Mobile HEALTH (MHEALTH) benchmark dataset across 343,195 samples from 10 participants. An exploratory analysis reveals distinct kinetic energy tiers across 12 physical activities and low cross-modal correlation between acceleration and angular velocity. We audit 615,648 missing values (7.80\% missingness) caused by wireless packet dropouts, finding that 92.4\% of bursts span a single sample (20\,ms). To prevent cross-activity contamination, we implement segment-bounded intra-run linear interpolation. We select three sensor sources spanning two modalities: Chest Accelerometer, Left-Ankle Accelerometer, and Right-Wrist Gyroscope. Using a subject-independent evaluation (Training: Subjects 1--7; Validation: Subject 8; Testing: Subjects 9--10), we compare three unimodal baselines against two multimodal fusion strategies: Data/Feature-Level Early Fusion and Decision-Level Late Soft-Voting. Under identical training budgets and cosine annealing schedules, Late Fusion achieves 95.56\% test accuracy and 0.9568 Macro-F1, improving upon the best unimodal baseline (Chest Accel: 88.48\% F1) by +7.20\% absolute F1 and resolving ambiguities between walking, climbing stairs, and upper-limb gestures. Finally, computational profiling demonstrates that on-sensor feature extraction reduces wireless telemetry bandwidth by 86.2\%, supported by an ethical risk governance matrix aligned with GDPR, HIPAA, and Australian Privacy Principles.
\end{abstract}

\subsection{Dataset Provenance and Architecture}
The Mobile HEALTH (MHEALTH) dataset, introduced by Ba{\~n}os et al.~\cite{banos2014mhealthdroid,banos2015sensor} and hosted on the UCI Machine Learning Repository~\cite{mhealth_uci_2014}, provides multimodal motion and physiological recordings for human activity benchmark analysis. Ten healthy volunteers (6 male, 4 female; aged 20--35) performed 12 physical activities in an out-of-laboratory setting.

Data was recorded using three Shimmer2 wearable inertial units attached to the chest, right wrist, and left ankle at a sampling frequency of $f_s = 50\,\text{Hz}$ ($\Delta t = 20\,\text{ms}$), which satisfies Nyquist sampling criteria for voluntary movement ($<20\,\text{Hz}$)~\cite{bulling2014tutorial}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Chest Sensor:} Measures trunk tilt, gravitational alignment, and respiratory/cardiac motion via a tri-axial accelerometer ($\pm 1.5\,\text{g}$, $\text{m/s}^2$) and 2-lead electrocardiogram (ECG, $\text{mV}$).
    \item \textbf{Left-Ankle Sensor:} Captures heel-strike impacts, propulsion, and stride frequency via a tri-axial accelerometer ($\pm 6\,\text{g}$), tri-axial gyroscope ($\pm 500^\circ/\text{s}$), and tri-axial magnetometer ($\pm 1\,\text{local field}$).
    \item \textbf{Right-Wrist Sensor:} Captures forearm rotational rate, arm swing, and gestures via tri-axial accelerometer, gyroscope, and magnetometer.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Modifications in the Adapted Teaching Dataset}
An inspection of the supplied archive (\texttt{MHEALTHDATASET\_MISSING}) identifies three key structural properties:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Removal of Null Class (Label 0):} In the original benchmark, 70\% of samples were unlabelled transition intervals between exercises. The adapted dataset retains only the 12 active target classes ($L_1$ to $L_{12}$).
    \item \textbf{Sample Distributions:} The dataset contains 343,195 rows across 10 subject files (\texttt{mHealth\_subject1.log} to \texttt{mHealth\_subject10.log}). Timed activities ($L_1$--$L_5, L_9$--$L_{11}$) contain exactly 30,720 samples across participants ($\approx 61.4\,\text{s}$ per subject at 50\,Hz). Repetition-based tasks show cadence variation ($L_6$: 28,315; $L_7$: 29,441; $L_8$: 29,337). Jumping ($L_{12}$) contains 10,342 samples ($\approx 20.7\,\text{s}$ per subject), reflecting brief, explosive execution (Table~\ref{tab:activities}).
    \item \textbf{Permuted Activity Sequences:} Activities were performed in different order across subjects (e.g., Subject 1 finishes with $L_5$, Subject 2 with $L_9$, and Subject 8 swaps $L_2$ and $L_3$). Consequently, preprocessing must not assume chronological uniformity across files.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Exploratory Data Analysis: Kinematics and Synergy}
We examine multi-axis kinematics across activity regimes to characterize pattern separability and sensor synergy.

\subsubsection{Signal Dynamics Across Activity Regimes}
Figure~\ref{fig:waveforms} contrasts raw sensor waveforms across three physical regimes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Static Posture (Standing, $L_1$):} Signals show stationary profiles dominated by gravity. The Chest Accelerometer records $\approx -9.8\,\text{m/s}^2$ along the vertical axis, while Ankle and Wrist sensors register near-zero dynamic deviation ($<0.05\,\text{m/s}^2$) and low angular rates ($<2.5^\circ/\text{s}$).
    \item \textbf{Periodic Locomotion (Walking, $L_4$):} Displays sinusoidal patterns at a gait frequency of $\approx 1.8\,\text{Hz}$. Ankle acceleration exhibits dynamic swings ($[-15, +25]\,\text{m/s}^2$) from cyclic heel-strike impacts, while the Wrist Gyroscope captures smooth angular velocity oscillations ($[-100, +80]^\circ/\text{s}$) from arm swing.
    \item \textbf{Explosive Impact (Jumping, $L_{12}$):} Features intermittent landing spikes ($>30\,\text{m/s}^2$) on the ankle, followed by aerial micro-gravity phases ($\approx 0\,\text{m/s}^2$) and high-frequency wrist rotations ($>250^\circ/\text{s}$).
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{Energy Stratification and Inter-Subject Variance}
We compute instantaneous Vector Magnitude (VM) and Signal Magnitude Area (SMA) for each window:
\begin{align}
    \text{VM}(t) &= \sqrt{x(t)^2 + y(t)^2 + z(t)^2} \\
    \text{SMA} &= \frac{1}{W}\sum_{t=1}^W \sum_{k} |s_k(t)|
\end{align}
Figure~\ref{fig:energy_dist} illustrates four distinct kinetic tiers:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sedentary Tier ($L_1, L_2, L_3$):} Ankle acceleration reflects gravity ($9.81\,\text{m/s}^2$), and wrist angular rates remain below $5^\circ/\text{s}$.
    \item \textbf{Moderate Ambulation ($L_4, L_5, L_9$):} Ankle acceleration averages $11.5$--$14.2\,\text{m/s}^2$, while cycling ($L_9$) shows low ankle variance but elevated rotational rates.
    \item \textbf{Vigorous Locomotion ($L_{10}, L_{11}$):} Jogging and running generate high ankle kinetic energy ($18.5$--$27.0\,\text{m/s}^2$) and sustained wrist angular rates ($>120^\circ/\text{s}$).
    \item \textbf{Calisthenic Bursts ($L_6, L_7, L_8, L_{12}$):} Display anatomical decoupling. Frontal arm elevation ($L_7$) generates high wrist rotation ($>90^\circ/\text{s}$) while the lower limbs remain stationary. Jumping ($L_{12}$) produces high lower-limb shock with variable wrist movement.
\end{enumerate}
The stripplot points in Figure~\ref{fig:energy_dist} highlight inter-subject variability; for example, Subject 3 exhibits 28\% higher running acceleration than Subject 6, reflecting personal differences in cadence and stride mechanics.

\subsubsection{Cross-Modality Correlation and Complementarity}
Figure~\ref{fig:correlation} presents the Pearson correlation matrix across 17 sensor channels. Intra-sensor axes show strong collinearity (e.g., Ankle Accel-X vs Accel-Z, $r = 0.54$), but cross-modality correlations remain low:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Wrist Gyroscope channels exhibit $|r| < 0.15$ with Ankle and Chest Accelerometers, confirming that rotational velocity provides complementary information to linear acceleration.
    \item ECG Leads 1 and 2 show near-zero linear correlation ($|r| < 0.08$) with motion channels, operating on distinct physiological time scales.
\end{itemize}
This low redundancy indicates that combining orthogonal modalities expands decision boundaries and mitigates unimodal blind spots~\cite{castanedo2013review}.

\subsection{Quantification and Diagnosis of Missingness}
In wearable sensor networks, packet loss occurs via RF attenuation, body shadowing, and transmission collisions. In \texttt{MHEALTHDATASET\_MISSING}, missing entries appear as IEEE \texttt{NaN} values.

An audit reveals \textbf{615,648 missing values} across 343,195 time steps, yielding an overall missingness rate of \textbf{7.80\%} across the 23 sensor channels (Table~\ref{tab:missing_stats}). The activity label column contains zero missing values. Missingness varies across channels (Figure~\ref{fig:missing_preproc}(a)), from 1.13\% (Wrist Gyro-Y) to 14.67\% (Ankle Mag-Z), but remains uniform across participants (7.43\% to 7.52\%).

\subsubsection{Burst Length Analysis and Missingness Mechanism}
To determine the missingness mechanism, we analyze consecutive missing sample runs (burst length $\ell = t_{\text{end}} - t_{\text{start}} + 1$). Figure~\ref{fig:missing_preproc}(b) shows that \textbf{92.4\% of bursts consist of a single sample} ($\Delta t = 20\,\text{ms}$). Bursts of $\ell = 2$ samples comprise 5.3\%, with a maximum burst length of $\ell_{\max} = 7$ samples ($140\,\text{ms}$) across the entire dataset. The mean burst length is $1.09 \pm 0.32$ samples.

Because packet loss occurs independently of signal amplitude ($P(\text{missing} \mid \mathbf{x}, y) = P(\text{missing})$) and bursts are brief, the data adheres to the \textbf{Missing Completely at Random (MCAR)} mechanism~\cite{lara2013survey}.

\subsection{Segment-Bounded Intra-Run Interpolation}
Because voluntary human movement has mechanical bandwidth $<20\,\text{Hz}$, kinematic trajectories remain smooth over sub-150\,ms intervals. \textbf{Local linear interpolation} provides an accurate reconstruction:
\begin{equation}
    \hat{s}(t) = s(t_a) + \frac{t - t_a}{t_b - t_a}\left(s(t_b) - s(t_a)\right), \quad t_a < t < t_b
\end{equation}
where $t_a$ and $t_b$ are the nearest observed samples.

\textbf{Preventing Boundary Leakage:} Interpolating across raw file boundaries would mix adjacent activities (e.g., interpolating jumping impacts into stair climbing). To avoid boundary mixing, our pipeline enforces \textbf{segment-bounded intra-run grouping}:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Partition subject logs into contiguous activity runs: $\mathcal{R}_k = \{(\mathbf{x}_t, y_t) \mid y_t = y_{t-1}\}$.
    \item Execute linear interpolation strictly within each run $\mathcal{R}_k$.
    \item Resolve initial/final segment missing values via intra-segment boundary replication (\texttt{limit\_direction='both'}).
\end{enumerate}
Figure~\ref{fig:missing_preproc}(c) shows that this approach reconstructs acceleration waveforms without cross-activity leakage. Post-processing confirms \textbf{zero residual missing values} (Table~\ref{tab:missing_stats}).

\subsection{Temporal Segmentation and Feature Engineering}
Wearable signals must be windowed to capture temporal structure while supporting real-time inference.

\subsubsection{Window Size and Overlap}
We apply a sliding window of $W = 128$ samples ($T = 2.56\,\text{s}$ at $50\,\text{Hz}$) with 50\% overlap ($\Delta W = 64$ samples, $1.28\,\text{s}$):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Gait Periodicity:} Walking cadences range from 1.5 to 2.2 steps/s (stride time $\approx 0.9$--$1.3\,\text{s}$)~\cite{lara2013survey}. A 2.56-second window captures over two full strides, supporting statistical stationarity.
    \item \textbf{Radix-2 FFT Efficiency:} Setting $W = 128 = 2^7$ optimizes Fast Fourier Transform execution without padding artifacts.
    \item \textbf{Inference Latency:} A 1.28-second step provides responsive tracking suitable for health monitoring.
\end{enumerate}
Windows are extracted strictly within each activity run $\mathcal{R}_k$, avoiding boundary crossing and producing $N = 5,229$ segmented windows.

\subsubsection{Extracted Features}
For each 3-axis sensor stream $\mathbf{S} \in \mathbb{R}^{128 \times 3}$, we extract 53 features:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Time-Domain Statistics (33 dims):} Mean $\mu$, standard deviation $\sigma$, variance $\sigma^2$, min, max, peak-to-peak range, median, interquartile range (IQR), skewness, kurtosis, and root mean square (RMS) per axis.
    \item \textbf{Kinetic Energy (5 dims):} Mean, standard deviation, min, and max of the Vector Magnitude $\text{VM}(t)$, plus Signal Magnitude Area ($\text{SMA}$).
    \item \textbf{Axis Correlation (3 dims):} Pairwise Pearson coefficients $\rho_{xy}, \rho_{xz}, \rho_{yz}$.
    \item \textbf{Frequency-Domain Features (12 dims):} Computed via real-valued FFT ($N_{\text{bins}} = 65$, $\Delta f = 0.39\,\text{Hz}$). Extracts Spectral Energy $E$, Spectral Entropy $H$, Dominant Frequency $f_{\text{dom}}$, and Peak Amplitude per axis.
\end{itemize}
Extracting 53 features across three sensor sources yields a 159-dimensional multimodal feature space per window.

\subsubsection{Standardisation}
All features are standardized via Z-score scaling ($z = (x - \mu) / \sigma$). Parameters $\mu_{\text{train}}$ and $\sigma_{\text{train}}$ are computed \textbf{strictly on the Training split} (Subjects 1--7) and applied to Validation and Testing sets, ensuring no test set leakage.

\subsection{Sensor Selection and Modality Justification}
We select three sensor sources spanning two physical modalities:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Chest Accelerometer (Linear Acceleration, 3 axes; Cols 0--2):} Measures upper-body posture, inclination during waist bending ($L_6$), and vertical displacement during jumping ($L_{12}$).
    \item \textbf{Left-Ankle Accelerometer (Linear Acceleration, 3 axes; Cols 5--7):} Measures ground impact shocks and lower-limb cadence, separating walking ($L_4$), climbing stairs ($L_5$), and running ($L_{11}$).
    \item \textbf{Right-Wrist Gyroscope (Angular Velocity, 3 axes; Cols 17--19):} Measures forearm rotational rates, identifying arm elevations ($L_7$), waist twists, and arm swings during cycling and running.
\end{enumerate}
This selection provides full-body kinematic coverage across trunk, lower extremity, and upper extremity.

\subsection{Subject-Independent Experimental Design}
Randomly partitioning windows across subjects leaks personal biomechanical traits between splits, inflating performance estimates. We enforce a \textbf{Subject-Independent Partition}:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Training Set:} Subjects 1 to 7 ($N_{\text{train}} = 3,686$ windows, 70.5\%).
    \item \textbf{Validation Set:} Subject 8 ($N_{\text{val}} = 508$ windows, 9.7\%).
    \item \textbf{Test Set:} Subjects 9 and 10 ($N_{\text{test}} = 1,035$ windows, 19.8\%).
\end{itemize}
All models are evaluated on this identical test split under uniform preprocessing.

\subsection{Unimodal Baselines and Fusion Strategies}
We implement five model configurations in PyTorch:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Baseline A (Chest Accelerometer):} Unimodal MLP on 53 chest features.
    \item \textbf{Baseline B (Ankle Accelerometer):} Unimodal MLP on 53 ankle features.
    \item \textbf{Baseline C (Wrist Gyroscope):} Unimodal MLP on 53 wrist features.
    \item \textbf{Early Fusion (Strategy 1):} Concatenates features into $\mathbf{x}_{\text{early}} = [\mathbf{f}_{\text{chest}} \,\|\, \mathbf{f}_{\text{ankle}} \,\|\, \mathbf{f}_{\text{wrist}}] \in \mathbb{R}^{159}$. A wider MLP (Table~\ref{tab:models}) learns cross-modal interactions in its early layers.
    \item \textbf{Late Fusion (Strategy 2):} Combines probability outputs from the three trained unimodal models via validation-weighted soft voting:
    \begin{equation}
        \mathbf{p}_{\text{late}}(\mathbf{x}) = \sum_{m} w_m \mathbf{p}_m(\mathbf{x}), \quad \sum w_m = 1
    \end{equation}
    where weights reflect validation Macro-F1: $w_{\text{chest}} = 0.358$, $w_{\text{ankle}} = 0.395$, and $w_{\text{wrist}} = 0.247$.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Training and Convergence Diagnostics}
Addressing previous feedback regarding fixed budgets, all models were trained for 45 epochs using AdamW ($1 \times 10^{-4}$ weight decay), gradient clipping at 2.0, and Cosine Annealing learning rate decay ($10^{-3}$ to $10^{-5}$). Checkpoints were selected by peak validation Macro-F1.

Figure~\ref{fig:training_curves} confirms stable convergence:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Training and validation loss curves decrease smoothly and plateau after Epoch 25.
    \item Peak validation F1 occurs at Epoch 18 for Early Fusion (0.8624), Epoch 19 for Ankle (0.8370), Epoch 11 for Wrist (0.5235), and Epoch 8 for Chest (0.7575).
    \item Validation loss does not diverge, demonstrating regularisation effectiveness.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Per-Class Performance and Error Analysis}
Table~\ref{tab:per_class_f1} provides a per-class F1 breakdown across all 12 classes for all five models, resolving the omission flagged in Assignment 1.

Combined with Figure~\ref{fig:confusion_matrices}, these results show how fusion resolves unimodal errors:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Ambulatory Confusion ($L_4$ Walking vs $L_5$ Stairs):} Chest Accel achieves only 0.676 F1 on walking, confusing 31\% of windows with stairs due to similar vertical trunk motion. Ankle Accel achieves \textbf{1.000 F1} on walking because dorsiflexion and foot impact profiles differ on stairs. Early and Late Fusion both achieve \textbf{1.000 F1} on walking and stairs.
    \item \textbf{Upper-Limb Gestures ($L_6$ Waist Bends vs $L_7$ Arm Elevation):} Wrist Gyro achieves only $F_1 = 0.522$ on waist bends, as hands remain fixed relative to the trunk. Chest Accel achieves \textbf{1.000 F1} due to forward pitch. Conversely, Ankle Accel achieves only 0.739 F1 on arm elevation ($L_7$) because the lower body is motionless; Wrist Gyro scores 1.000 F1. Fusion resolves both cases, scoring \textbf{1.000 F1}.
    \item \textbf{Explosive Impacts ($L_{12}$ Jumping):} Wrist Gyro achieves $F_1 = 0.545$ on jumping, confusing arm recoil with general movement. Chest and Ankle Accelerometers record clear impact spikes ($F_1 = 1.000$), enabling fusion models to achieve \textbf{1.000 F1}.
    \item \textbf{Sitting ($L_2$) vs Standing ($L_1$):} The remaining confusion across models is between Sitting ($L_2$, F1 = 0.685) and Standing ($L_1$, F1 = 0.807). Because the trunk remains vertical ($1\,\text{g}$ along the vertical axis) and both sensors are stationary, distinction relies on subtle differences in posture and wrist rest angle. Fusion improves sitting F1 from 0.496 (chest baseline) to 0.685.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:roc_pr} shows macro-average ROC and Precision-Recall curves:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Early and Late Fusion achieve an Area Under the ROC Curve of $\text{AUC} = 0.999$ (Figure~\ref{fig:roc_pr}(a)), with true positive rates $>0.96$ at false positive rates below 0.01.

\subsection{Trade-offs: Early vs Late Fusion}
The fusion paradigms present distinct engineering trade-offs:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Early Fusion:} Permits the network to model joint cross-sensor dependencies from input features. However, it requires all sensors to be present; missing one node breaks the 159-dimensional vector.
    \item \textbf{Late Fusion:} Provides modularity and fault tolerance. Each unimodal network operates independently, allowing dynamic weight re-normalisation if a sensor disconnects. Late Fusion slightly exceeds Early Fusion on Macro-F1 (0.9568 vs 0.9537) by avoiding high-dimensional feature interference.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Computational Implications for Edge Deployment}
Deploying multimodal inference on resource-constrained microcontrollers (e.g., ARM Cortex-M4) introduces specific constraints:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Memory Footprint:} Unimodal MLPs require 16,332 parameters ($\approx 65.3\,\text{KB}$ in float32). Early Fusion scales to 76,172 parameters ($\approx 304.7\,\text{KB}$), while Late Fusion combines three networks totaling 48,996 parameters ($\approx 196.0\,\text{KB}$) (Figure~\ref{fig:computational_tradeoffs}(a)), fitting within typical 512\,KB SRAM budgets.
    \item \textbf{Inference Latency:} Benchmarking 1,000 single-window passes on CPU yields \textbf{0.031\,ms for Early Fusion} and \textbf{0.097\,ms for Late Fusion} (Figure~\ref{fig:computational_tradeoffs}(b)). Late Fusion is slower due to evaluating three models sequentially, but 0.097\,ms uses $<0.01\%$ of the 1,280\,ms window budget.
    \item \textbf{Bandwidth and Energy:} Bluetooth LE radio transmission consumes $\approx 15$--$25\,\text{mW}$, whereas microcontroller computation requires $<2\,\text{mW}$~\cite{lara2013survey}. Transmitting raw 50\,Hz data requires $128 \times 3 \times 4 = 1,536\,\text{bytes}$ per window. Extracting 53 features locally reduces telemetry to $53 \times 4 = 212\,\text{bytes}$---\textbf{an 86.2\% bandwidth saving}.
    \item \textbf{Clock Synchronisation:} Early fusion requires sub-millisecond time alignment across nodes. Late fusion is more tolerant of arrival jitter, as unimodal decisions can be aggregated with timestamp buffers.
\end{enumerate}

Deploying continuous wearable sensing in clinical environments presents key ethical, legal, and operational considerations (Table~\ref{tab:ethics_matrix}).

\subsection{Biometric Privacy Frameworks}
Wearable motion data contains identifying signatures. Prior biomechanical research indicates that tri-axial gait acceleration can fingerprint individuals with $>95\%$ accuracy, while ECG traces expose cardiac conditions and autonomic state~\cite{bulling2014tutorial}.

These data streams require structured governance:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{GDPR:} Under GDPR Article~4(14), gait acceleration constitutes biometric data, while cardiac measurements fall under \textbf{Special Category Health Data (Article~9)}, requiring explicit consent (Article~6(1)(a) and 9(2)(a)). Article~22 restricts automated decision-making without clinical oversight.
    \item \textbf{HIPAA:} In clinical settings, wearable telemetry constitutes Protected Health Information (PHI), requiring encryption under the HIPAA Security Rule (45 CFR Part 164).
    \item \textbf{Australian Privacy Principles (APPs):} Under the Privacy Act 1988, biometric and health data are classified as \textbf{Sensitive Information} (APP~3), requiring express consent and secure data lifecycle handling (APP~11).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Algorithmic Bias and Demographic Generalisation}
The MHEALTH dataset was collected from 10 healthy young adult volunteers (aged 20--35). This demographic profile creates deployment risks:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Age Biomechanics:} Older adults exhibit reduced stride length, slower cadence, and altered balance sway. Models trained on young cohorts risk classifying normal elderly gait as abnormal.
    \item \textbf{Mobility Impairments:} Post-stroke hemiplegia or Parkinsonian gait generate asymmetric motions that depart from healthy volunteer patterns, increasing false alarms in monitoring systems.
\end{itemize}
Production deployments require domain adaptation and demographic validation across age and mobility cohorts prior to clinical application.

\section{Limitations and Future Improvements}
\label{sec:limitations}

Key technical limitations include:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Sitting vs Standing Resolution:} Confusion between Sitting ($L_2$) and Standing ($L_1$) remains the primary residual error (Table~\ref{tab:per_class_f1}). Adding a thigh-mounted sensor would directly capture femoral tilt ($90^\circ$ difference), resolving this ambiguity.
    \item \textbf{Deep Temporal Architectures:} While engineered features support efficient edge inference, future work could evaluate end-to-end models such as DeepConvLSTM~\cite{ordonez2016deep} or Attention Transformers~\cite{radu2018multimodal} directly on interpolated raw streams.
    \item \textbf{Dropout Stress-Testing:} Empirical testing under simulated packet loss (10\%--50\%) would further quantify the fault tolerance of the late-fusion ensemble.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}
This report demonstrated an end-to-end multimodal activity classification pipeline on the adapted MHEALTH dataset. Using segment-bounded intra-run interpolation, we resolved 615,648 missing values without cross-activity leakage. Selecting three sensor sources across acceleration and gyroscope modalities and evaluating on unseen test participants, Late Fusion achieved 95.56\% accuracy and 0.9568 Macro-F1, eliminating unimodal ambiguities between walking, stairs, and calisthenics. Computational profiling demonstrated an 86.2\% telemetry bandwidth reduction via local feature extraction, supported by a governance framework aligned with international privacy standards.
